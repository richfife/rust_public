# Zebra Puzzle Generator in Rust

## What is a Zebra Puzzle?

A Zebra puzzle (sometimes called an Einstein Puzzle) goes something like this:

"There is a street with X houses.  Each house has a different colored door and nationality and personal taste of its owner."

followed by a list of clues like:

*Clue  1: The house with a blue door is left of the house with a fish as a pet.<br>
Clue  2: The house with sushi as a favorite food is right of the house with a maroon door.<br>
Clue  3: The house with a red door also has skiing as a favorite sport.<br>
Clue  4: The house with pasta as a favorite food is left of the house with stone construction.<br>
Clue  5: House number 5 has steak as a favorite food.<br>
Clue  6: The house with stone construction is left of the house with adobe construction.<br>
Clue  7: The house with soup as a favorite food is right of the house with steel construction.<br>
Clue  8: The house with adobe construction is left of the house with swimming as a favorite sport.<br>
Clue  9: The house with tennis as a favorite sport is right of the house with an aardvark as a pet.<br>
Clue 10: The house with a turtle as a pet also has an orange door.<br>
Clue 11: The house with a monkey as a pet is right of the house with a hamster as a pet.<br>
Clue 12: The house with brick construction is right of the house with basketball as a favorite sport.<br>
Clue 13: The house with sandwiches as a favorite food is left of the house with concrete construction.<br>
Clue 14: The house with a blue door is left of the house with concrete construction.*

Finally followed by one or more questions:

*Which house has a green door?<br>
Which house has soccer as a favorite sport?*

At which point you determine the answer based on the clues.  It should be possible to determine the choices of all the houses completely using just the clues.  In this case:

    Solution:
    House number   1           2           3           4           5
    Sport          Skiing      Soccer      Basketball  Swimming    Tennis
    Pet            Hamster     Monkey      Turtle      Aardvark    Fish
    Construction   Steel       Stone       Adobe       Brick       Concrete
    Food           Pasta       Soup        Sushi       Sandwiches  Steak    
    Door           Red         Maroon      Orange      Blue        Green

This example was generated by the app.

## How do I generate one?
Running this app with no parameters will output a new puzzle to stdout.

Parameters:

    Options:
          --houses <HOUSES>
              Number of times to greet (optional, defaults to 1) [default: 6]
          --classes <CLASSES>
              [default: 6]
          --question-count <QUESTION_COUNT>
              [default: 2]
          --config-file <CONFIG_FILE>
              [default: config.json]

It needs a "config.json" file (defaults to the current dir) to get a list of names of attributes from.

You can also (optionally) set defaults for the number of house, types of things and questions asked in the json:

    {
        "houses": 5,
        "class_count": 5,
        "question_count": 2,
    
        "class_names" :
        [
            {
                "class": "door",
                "variants": ["a red", "a green", "a blue", "a white", "a yellow", "a black", "an orange", "a purple", "a maroon"]
            },
            {
                "class": "owner",
                "variants": ["a Norwegian", "an English", "a Swedish", "a Danish", "a German", "a Japanese", "an Italian", "a Spanish", "a Belgian"]
            },
    ...
            }
        ]
    }

Output will be similar to the example above (which was generated using the app).

## Glossary:

**House**: The number of the house on the street (0, 1, 2, 3...).

**Class**: The type of characteristic of a house (Door color, pet, favorite food...).

**Choice**: An actual choice a resident might make (Blue, dog, sandwiches...).

**Pair**: A combination of a class and a choice represented like (Class:Choice).  So(2:3) means "Choice 3 of class 2".

**Clue**: Some true thing about the solution.  "The house with stone construction is left of the house with adobe construction." or "House number 5 has steak as a favorite food.".

During the construction process, these are kept as a pair of pairs.

**Remember the choice indices in the solution are always the same as the house index!**

*(House 1 is left of house 2)*<br>
((1,2), (2,3)) means: "The house with choice 1 of class 2 is left of the house with choice 2 of class 2".

**Slot**: A class for a house.  The color of the door of house 3, for instance.

## Design approach
At the top level, the first decision was fairly obvious:  Represent the houses using numeric indices instead of strings and only map the indices into phrases at the very end.
Another choice occurred to me that turned out to simplify the process greatly: I could declare the choices in final solution to be always be the same as the house index.

Like so:

    House number   0          1           2           3           4
    Class 0        (0:0)      (0:1)       (0:2)       (0:3)       (0:4)
    Class 1        (1:0)      (1:1)       (1:2)       (1:3)       (1:4)
    Class 2        (2:0)      (2:1)       (2:2)       (2:3)       (2:4)
    ...

### The solution is always numerically the same.

The second field in all of the pairs is always equal to the house.  The randomness of the solution would come entirely from shuffling the terms before printing them out.

The advantage of this is, during the process of generating clues to make wrong answers illegal, it makes it much, much easier to find which pairs are in the wrong place and how they relate to the right place.

## Coding approach

### Choose the final question(s)

First, we choose what the final questions will be; "Which house has the red door" for instance.  At this point, we just record it as a house number and class number (the choice is always the same as the house, so we don't need to record that separately).

We want to know this first thing so we know to avoid this slot while generating clues.

There is a rule here that no two questions can reference the same class.  You can't have "Which house has the green door?" and "Which house has the blue door?" at the same time because these pairings will always be ambiguous.

This limits the number of questions to the number of classes.  Sorry.

### Initial clue set

The first step is to generate a set of clues that will mention all of the pair positions exactly once (not including the final question slots).

At this point, we start randomly generating possible clues ("The house with a gerbil is left of the house with a red door" for instance) and checking to make sure they don't conflict with earlier clues.  We do this until we make 100 straight clues in a row that conflict with previous ones.

Once that happens, we finish the list by just generating clues for the untouched houses ("House 3 has a Swedish resident" for instance) to get complete coverage.

### Where are we now?

About 10% of the time, we've already come up with a set of clues that unambiguosly lead to a solution. That is, of course, not good enough.  We will need to identify wrong but legal solutions and add additional clues until only one legal solution remains.

A couple of questions need to be answered:

1) How many solutions exist (hopefully exactly 1)?
2) What are the best (fewest) clues to push that will make the legal but wrong solutions illegal?

### Solver (Solve problem #1)

Recurse through the clue list:

1) Apply the clue to each house left to right.
2) If there is a conflict with a previous clue application, move to the next house.
3) Otherwise, record the current proposed set of choices and recurse to the next clue.
4) If we run out of clues, record this as a legal solution by adding it a list of them.

If there is only one legal solution, we're done.  Go to "Outputting result".

Otherwise...

### Eliminating legal wrong answers (Solve problem #2)

This is trickiest logic in the generator.

We want to create a puzzle that establishes a unique solution using the fewest clues possible. To do that, at each iteration, we want to find the clue that invalidates the greatest number of wrong but legal solutions as possible and add it to the list, repeating until only the right solution remains legal.

So... how do we evaluate the effectiveness of a potential new clue?

Remember:  A clue is two class/choice pairs.

Also remember: the "correct" solution has each choice index equal to the house it is in.  The choice index in all of the classes of house 2 are always 2.

This creates an opportunity:  If we look at all the possible clues for a wrong answay, the relative relations will sometimes be wrong.

If a legal clue for a wrong solution is something like "(1:2) is left of (3:4)", we know that this can't be true of the right solution (4 is two greater than 2; it should be only one greater).

What we do is we count the number of times legal clues ((1:2)-(3:3) for instance) can be applied to the list of legal (but possibly wrong) solutions.  The clue that is legal in the fewest possible cases will, inversely, invalidate the greated number of legal but wrong solutions.

We push that clue on the stack an run the solver code again, but on the second and furthe solver iterations, we only check the legal solutions from the previous run against the new clue.  We don't need to start from the beginning again.

This goes on until only the correct solution remains.  It seems, to my eye, to converge by about a factor of 10 per clue on average.

### Outputting result

The only interesting this here is that, since the solution is always predetermined numerically (the choices are always equal to the house #), we randomize the names of them in order to make each puzzle appear unique to the human solver.
